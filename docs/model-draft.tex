\section{Indices and dimensions}

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}llX@{}}
\toprule
 & Range & \\
\midrule
$j$ & $1,\dots,P$ & model parameters \\
$q$ & $1,\dots,Q$ & model species \\
$r$ & $1,\dots,M$ & readouts \\
$s$ & $1,\dots,S$ & scenarios \\
$c$ & $1,\dots,C$ & study cohorts; $s(c)$ is the scenario cohort $c$ was run under \\
$k$ & $1,\dots,K_{rc}$ & statistics that cohort $c$ reports for readout $r$ \\
$i$ & $1,\dots,N$ & simulated patients \\
$\Rset_c$ & $\subseteq\{1,\dots,M\}$ & the readouts that cohort $c$ reports \\
$K_c$ & $\sum_{r\in\Rset_c}K_{rc}$ & the rows that cohort $c$ contributes \\
$B$ & $\in\Bset$ & a block: the cohorts that have to be drawn together \\
$K_B$ & $\sum_{c\in B}K_c$ & the rows of block $B$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\noindent
$\theta\in\mathbb{R}^P_{+}$ are the parameters and $\vartheta=\log\theta$. A
\emph{target} is a $(r,c)$ pair. Readouts are on the log scale. $n_c$ is the real
cohort size, as from the actual data it comes from. A target names a row, not a
unit of independence, and neither does a cohort: the block is that. A cohort is
the unit that owns an $n$, an eligibility rule and a level $c_{rc}$, and most
blocks hold exactly one.

\section{Elicitation}
\label{sec:elicit}

Two inputs are elicited: the spread centre $\omega_0$ that \eqref{eq:omegaassumed}
builds on, and the loadings $\Lambda$ that \eqref{eq:Rfactor} turns into a
between-patient correlation. Nothing measures either. They are stated in the same
form, and that form is the contract a reader is asked to check.

A value comes from a small set of bins rather than as a free number, since an
elicitation does not support three significant figures and printing them invites
a reader to believe them. A bin is named for the warrant for its value rather
than for the value, because the same number gets claimed for incompatible reasons
and the name is what lets a reader tell which claim is being made. Each is $O(P)$
claims about single parameters rather than $P(P-1)/2$ about pairs. Every
assignment is recorded per parameter alongside the level it came from, so any
width or correlation traces back to the claim that produced it.

\paragraph{Roles: how wide a parameter is.}
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabularx}{0.95\textwidth}{@{}llrX@{}}
\toprule
\texttt{patient\_trait} & $0.70$ & $18$ & clinically observed to vary from patient to patient \\
\texttt{lumped} & $0.50$ & $50$ & stands in for structure the model does not resolve, so its spread is the coarseness varying and not an observation \\
\texttt{default} & $0.35$ & $172$ & a biological quantity with no reason to be special \\
\texttt{packing\_limit} & $0.15$ & $12$ & a geometric ceiling \\
\texttt{material\_constant} & $0.10$ & $4$ & a property of matter, the same in every person \\
\texttt{structural} & $0.05$ & $15$ & a numerical gate, not a physical quantity, so varying it across patients has no meaning \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
The third column is how the $271$ parameters currently fall. $0.35$ is a $36\%$
CV and is what a parameter gets when nothing more specific is known; $0.50$ is
the log-midpoint $\sqrt{0.35\cdot0.70}$, so it privileges neither neighbour. A
role says how wide and never on what scale: the log or logit margin of
\eqref{eq:mu} is orthogonal to it, and four parameters carry the logit.

The gaps are load-bearing and not only the values. $\tau_u$ of
\eqref{eq:omegaassumed} is a quarter of the narrowest gap,
$\log(0.70/0.50)=0.336$, leaving four standard deviations to the nearest role
boundary, so the fitted pattern cannot move a parameter out of the role it was
assigned: $\tau_u = 0.085$. Changing a value in the ladder changes that
derivation.

\paragraph{Loadings: what a parameter varies with.} A parameter loads on at most
a few declared latent patient axes, with a magnitude and a sign:
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabularx}{0.95\textwidth}{@{}llX@{}}
\toprule
\texttt{primary} & $0.8$ & the axis explains $64\%$ of a patient's variation in that parameter \\
\texttt{secondary} & $0.5$ & $25\%$ \\
\texttt{weak} & $0.3$ & $9\%$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
A parameter with no declared loading is uncorrelated with the rest, and that is
the default rather than a gap. The communality $\sum_k \Lambda_{jk}^2$ is capped
at $0.81$, which keeps $R$ strictly positive definite so that its Cholesky cannot
fail and no nearest-correlation projection is ever called; a projection would
rewrite entries far from the violation it was called for, and what was reviewed
would stop being what was shipped. The cap also asserts that no modelled
parameter is fully determined by the axes. Axes are declared, and a loading
naming one that is not is dropped and reported rather than creating an axis, so a
typo cannot become a factor that nothing else loads on.

\section{The model}

\paragraph{Population.}
\begin{equation}
\vartheta_i \mid \mu,\omega \;\sim\; \Ncal_P\big(\mu,\ \Omega\big),
\qquad
\Omega \;=\; D_\omega\,R\,D_\omega,
\qquad
D_\omega=\diag(\omega),
\label{eq:pop}
\end{equation}
with $R$ a fixed correlation matrix. Realised with common random numbers, so
that $\vartheta_i$ is a smooth function of $(\mu,\omega)$:
\begin{equation}
\vartheta_i \;=\; \mu + D_\omega L_R z_i,
\qquad
z_i \stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim} \Ncal_P(0,I_P) \ \text{ drawn once},
\qquad
R = L_R L_R\Tr .
\label{eq:crn}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Between-patient correlation.} $R$ is elicited rather than inherited
from stage 1. That posterior correlation is epistemic, and reading it here would
assert something else, that a patient high in one parameter is high in the other.
Nothing measures the latter, so it is elicited the way the widths are, as a claim
about what drives a parameter. Each parameter loads on at most a few latent
patient axes:
\begin{equation}
z_{ij} \;=\; \sum_{k} \Lambda_{jk} f_{ik} \;+\; \psi_j e_{ij},
\qquad
\sum_{k}\Lambda_{jk}^2 + \psi_j^2 = 1,
\qquad
R \;=\; \Lambda\Lambda\Tr + \diag(\psi^2),
\label{eq:Rfactor}
\end{equation}
with $f_{ik}$ and $e_{ij}$ iid standard normal, so \eqref{eq:Rfactor} is a
construction of the $z_i$ of \eqref{eq:crn}. The loading ladder and the
communality cap are in \S\ref{sec:elicit}.

\paragraph{Mechanism.} Simulate patient $i$ under scenario $s$ and read off the
species at the measured time:
\begin{equation}
y_i(s) \;=\; g\big(\vartheta_i;\,s\big) \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^Q_{+} .
\label{eq:mech}
\end{equation}
A readout composes those species into the scalar a study reports, on the log
scale:
\begin{equation}
x_{irc} \;=\; h_r\big(y_i(1),\dots,y_i(S)\big).
\label{eq:readout}
\end{equation}
Most readouts use only $y_i(s(c))$. A fold change is a contrast between two
scenarios in the same patient, which \eqref{eq:crn} is what makes meaningful, so
$h_r$ takes the whole scenario set.

\paragraph{Discrepancy.} Two kinds of error, distinguished by where they enter
and therefore by how they propagate:
\begin{equation}
\tilde x_{irc}
\;=\;
\kappa_r\Big(h_r\big(
\underbrace{e^{\beta}\odot y_i(s(c))}_{\text{mechanism, upstream of }h_r}
\big) - c_{rc}\Big)
\;+\; c_{rc} \;+\;
\gamma_r ,
\qquad
\underbrace{\gamma_r = Z_r\Tr a,
\quad
\log\kappa_r = Z_r\Tr b}_{\text{measurement, downstream of }h_r} .
\label{eq:disc}
\end{equation}
$\gamma$ and $\kappa$ are the location and scale halves of an affine map on the
readout, pivoting about $c_{rc}$.

$\beta\in\mathbb{R}^Q$ is a log-scale bias on each \emph{species}, shared across
readouts and scenarios. It enters upstream of $h_r$, so it propagates through the
composition on its own: a ratio inherits $\beta_{q_1}-\beta_{q_2}$, a percentage
inherits $\beta_q - \beta_{\text{nuc}}$, a sum inherits a weighted combination.

$a$ and $b$ are measurement effects on the \emph{readout}, entering downstream of
$h_r$, so they do not propagate. $Z_r$ holds attributes of the readout that have
no mechanistic counterpart: assay modality, and whether the quantity is a
density, a fraction or a concentration.

$\beta=0$, $a=0$, $b=0$ is a model with no discrepancy, that is, a model that is not biased in scale or location on any of the observed species.

\paragraph{The pivot.} $c_{rc}$ is the level readout $r$ sits at in cohort $c$,
taken as that study's own median at $(\mu_0,\omega_0)$ under its own eligibility
rule. It is fixed once and not rebuilt when $V_B$ moves to another $\phi_0$:
$\gamma_r$ is the intercept at the reference level, so changing $c_{rc}$
redefines the prior on $a$.

The plug-in is the no-discrepancy point $\beta=0,a=0,b=0$, where
\eqref{eq:disc} is the identity and $\tilde x = x$ whatever $c_{rc}$ is. So the
pivot is a median of $x$ and does not need a pivot to define it.

At $c_{rc}=0$ the map is $\kappa_r h_r + \gamma_r$, and $h_r$ is a log quantity
with a level well away from zero, so $\kappa_r$ shifts the readout as well as
stretching it. Pivoting at $c_{rc}$ leaves $\kappa_r$ acting on deviations from
the study's own level, so it is a spread term within the cohort and $\gamma_r$
carries the location alone.

The study index adds no parameters: the offset the map applies,
$\gamma_r + c_{rc}(1-\kappa_r)$, differs between cohorts only through levels that
are already fixed.

\paragraph{The cohort vector of one patient.} Stack the corrected readouts that
cohort $c$ reports, for a single patient:
\begin{equation}
\tilde x_{ic} \;=\; \big(\tilde x_{irc}\big)_{r\in\Rset_c}
\;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{|\Rset_c|} .
\label{eq:stack}
\end{equation}
Built from $\tilde x$ and not $x$: $\beta$ enters inside $h_r$, so
\eqref{eq:disc} does not factor through \eqref{eq:readout}.

\paragraph{Cohort selection.} Specified, not implemented: no cohort in the
corpus declares a criterion, so $w^{(c)}\equiv1$ throughout and the fit reads an
unweighted cloud. What follows is the design a declared criterion would be read
into, and it is the reason the row functionals are stated as weighted ones.

Where cohort $c$ reports an eligibility criterion,
$w^{(c)}_i\in[0,1]$ is a smooth indicator of whether patient $i$ meets it, and
$w^{(c)}_i \equiv 1$ otherwise. A criterion stated as an interval
$[\ell_1,\ell_2]$ on readout $r$ gives
\begin{equation}
w^{(c)}_i \;=\;
\sigma\!\big((\tilde x_{irc}-\ell_1)/h_w\big)\;
\sigma\!\big((\ell_2-\tilde x_{irc})/h_w\big),
\label{eq:elig}
\end{equation}
with $\sigma$ the logistic and $h_w$ a fixed bandwidth. Write $\tilde F_{c}$ for
the $w^{(c)}$-weighted empirical distribution of $\{\tilde x_{ic}\}_{i=1}^N$ on
$\mathbb{R}^{|\Rset_c|}$, and $\tilde F_{rc}$ for its $r$-th marginal.

The criterion is stated in reported units, so it reads $\tilde x$ rather than
$x$. Eligibility therefore depends on $a$, $b$ and $\beta$, and is not fixed
in $\phi$.

Cohorts drawn together must share $w^{(c)}$: one draw serves them all, so
criteria that select differently leave it undefined. The unit is the joint draw
and not the block, since cohorts joined only by a shared row are disjoint people
and each reads its own. Evaluating a shared criterion once per cohort would not
do: $\tilde x_{irc}$ differs across cohorts through the scenario and through
$c_{rc}$, so the same rule would select different patients. It is evaluated once,
on the cohort the criterion enrolled. In practice these are arms and timepoints
of one trial and that is the baseline.

\paragraph{Statistics.} Cohort $c$ reports statistics $T_{rc1},\dots,T_{rcK_{rc}}$
for readout $r$, each a functional of a sample of $n_c$ patients. The model
computes the same functionals of samples of the same size drawn from its own
predicted population:
\begin{equation}
\tau_{rck}(\phi) \;=\;
\mathbb{E}\Big[\,T_{rck}\big(\tilde F^{\,\ast}_{rc}\big)\,\Big],
\qquad
\tilde F^{\,\ast}_{rc} = \text{an } n_c\text{-sample from } \tilde F_{rc}.
\label{eq:stat}
\end{equation}
Order the rows of cohort $c$ by $r\in\Rset_c$ and then by $k$ within $r$, and
stack them into $\tau_c(\phi)\in\mathbb{R}^{K_c}$, and the reported values the
same way into $\widehat T_c$. Concatenating over $c\in B$ in a fixed cohort order
gives $\tau_B$ and $\widehat T_B$. Since $\tilde F_{rc}$ is a marginal of $\tilde
F_c$, the joint form changes $V_B$ and leaves $\tau$ alone.

Whatever the source printed is a row, in the form it printed it. Re-expressing a
quartile pair as a width would discard the asymmetry between $\hat q_{0.75}-\hat
q_{0.5}$ and $\hat q_{0.5}-\hat q_{0.25}$, which is most of what a cohort says
about the shape of the pushforward and so about $\Omega$.

The expectation in \eqref{eq:stat} is what makes $\tau$ the mean of
\eqref{eq:obs} rather than the quantity of interest. A statistic computed from
$n_c$ patients is not the functional of the population it came from, and the gap
closes as $n_c$ grows, not as the predicted cloud does. For quantiles it runs
inward on both sides, so predicting the population functional instead would meet
the data by shrinking $\Omega$: at a log-scale spread of $1$ and $n_c=9$ the
population lower quartile is $-0.674$ against a reported $-0.570$, which is
recovered at a spread of $0.845$. The population functional is the deliverable
and is read off the posterior in \eqref{eq:vpop}, never off a row.

\paragraph{Smoothing.} $\tau$ has to be differentiable in $\phi$ and the reported
functionals are not: a quantile picks out one order statistic, so its gradient
rests on a single patient and jumps when two patients swap rank. Quantiles are
taken with a Beta kernel in cumulative-weight space instead,
\begin{equation}
\hat q(p) \;=\; \frac{\sum_i k_i\,x_{(i)}}{\sum_i k_i},
\qquad
k_i = w_{(i)}\,f_{\text{B}}\big(c_i;\,\varkappa,\,n_c-\varkappa+1\big),
\qquad
c_i = \frac{\sum_{l\le i} w_{(l)} - \tfrac12 w_{(i)}}{\sum_l w_l},
\label{eq:smoothq}
\end{equation}
with $\varkappa$ the order statistic the cohort's estimator selects. Sort $n_c$
draws from any distribution and the rank of the $\varkappa$-th in it is
$\text{Beta}(\varkappa,n_c-\varkappa+1)$ whatever its shape, so this is exactly
the expectation \eqref{eq:stat} asks for and differentiability comes with it.
Centre $\varkappa/(n_c+1)$ and width $\sqrt{p(1-p)/n_c}$ are set by $n_c$ rather
than chosen.

$\varkappa$ is a convention: $(n_c-1)p+1$ in R and numpy, $(n_c+1)p$ in SPSS,
centring at $0.30$ and $0.25$ when $n_c=9$. No source records which it used, so
declare it per cohort and report the sensitivity.

A width, a standard deviation or an interquartile range, is not an order
statistic and \eqref{eq:smoothq} does not reach it. It enters as its log, and
that log is what makes $\kappa_r$ an additive shift on the row.

\paragraph{Observation.} One block is one block:
\begin{equation}
\widehat T_{B} \;\sim\; \Ncal_{K_{B}}\big(\tau_{B}(\phi),\ V_{B}\big),
\qquad
\widehat T_{B} \perp \widehat T_{B'} \ \text{ for } B\neq B' .
\label{eq:obs}
\end{equation}
Under the ordering of \eqref{eq:stat}, the diagonal blocks of $V_B$ are the
per-target covariances and the off-diagonal blocks carry the dependence between
readouts, whether of one cohort or of two that were drawn together.

\paragraph{Blocks.} Two cohorts sit in one $B$ when a row depends on both. Either
one row is built from both, as a contrast across arms is, or they share people,
as a subgroup reported beside the whole does. $\Bset$ is the partition into
connected components under the two relations; $|B|=1$ recovers a cohort.

Which relation put them there decides the draw. Cohorts joined by a shared row
are disjoint sets of people and are resampled independently. Cohorts that share
people are resampled once from the block's patient set, each cohort's rows
evaluated on its own members.

Sharing need not be nesting: 16 evaluable baseline biopsies against day-21 arms
of 9 and 10 admit neither a common $n$ nor a containment. The block carries the
union and each cohort keeps its $n_c$. Merging cohorts is the total-overlap case.

Sharing has to be counted to be drawn, as the counts on the Venn regions of the
block's cohorts. Where two studies are known to share patients but neither
prints which, the draw falls back to independent, $V_B$ understates those
off-diagonals and the fit is overconfident there. Record the block as uncounted
and bound the effect by refitting without the second study.

\paragraph{Study effect.} A study shifts its own location rows together:
\begin{equation}
\widehat T_B \mid \eta \;\sim\; \Ncal_{K_B}\Big(
  \tau_B(\phi) + \textstyle\sum_{s} \eta_s\,\mathbf{1}^{\mathrm{loc}}_{B,s},
  \ V_B\Big),
\qquad
\eta_s \stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim} \Ncal\big(0,\ \tau_\eta^2\big),
\label{eq:studyeff}
\end{equation}
with $\mathbf{1}^{\mathrm{loc}}_{B,s}\in\{0,1\}^{K_B}$ the indicator of the
location rows of block $B$ that come from study $s$. The index is the study and
not the cohort, since arms of one trial share a lab and a patient stream. A width
row is untouched: $\eta$ is a level effect and \eqref{eq:disc} already gives
widths their own scale term.

Gaussian and additive, so \eqref{eq:studyeff} marginalises exactly:
\begin{equation}
\widehat T_B \;\sim\; \Ncal_{K_B}\Big(\tau_B(\phi),\
  V_B + \tau_\eta^2 \textstyle\sum_s
  \mathbf{1}^{\mathrm{loc}}_{B,s}\mathbf{1}^{\mathrm{loc}\Tr}_{B,s}\Big).
\label{eq:studymarg}
\end{equation}
So $\eta$ adds no coordinate to $\phi$, and the addition is free of $\phi$ and
leaves $V_B$ frozen. A study reporting one row has that row's variance raised by
$\tau_\eta^2$; a study reporting many keeps its within-study contrasts at full
weight and discounts only their common level, which is the correction the corpus
needs, since one study supplies $59$ of the $138$ rows.

$\tau_\eta$ is fixed. A free $\eta_s$ is not available at this corpus: $22$
studies against $138$ rows, $3$ of them reporting a single row that a free shift
would fit exactly, and $8$ directions of $\eta$ already spanned by $Z$.

\paragraph{Sampling covariance.} $V_{B}$ is the covariance of the reported
statistics of block $B$ under resampling of its patients. It has two
ingredients. The first is a bootstrap from the predicted population at a plug-in
$\phi_0$, held fixed thereafter:
\begin{equation}
V^{\text{boot}}_{B}
\;=\;
\Cov_{\ast}\!\Big[\,T_{B}\big(\tilde F^{\ast}_{B}\big)\,\Big]_{\phi_0},
\qquad
\tilde F^{\ast}_{B}
= \text{resample of } n_c \text{ patients from } \tilde F_{c}, \ c\in B,
\label{eq:V}
\end{equation}
where a resampled patient carries its whole vector $\tilde x_{ic}$ from
\eqref{eq:stack}. Drawing patients rather than readout values is what moves the
dependence structure into $V_B$. Where cohorts of $B$ share people the draw is
common: a patient drawn once carries the same $\tilde x_i$ into every cohort it
belongs to, and that is the only source of across-cohort correlation. It is
predicted at $\phi_0$, not measured.

The second is $E_B$, from the emulator $\widehat g$ standing in for $g$. Define
it on the statistics and not on the readouts, because the map from a readout
error to a statistic error depends on the functional. Draw $L$ held-out clouds
$z^{(\ell)}$ at $\phi_0$, evaluate each both ways, and split the difference into
its mean and what fluctuates around it:
\begin{equation}
d^{(\ell)}_B
\;=\;
\tau^{\widehat g}_B\big(\phi_0;\,z^{(\ell)}\big)
\;-\;
\tau^{g}_B\big(\phi_0;\,z^{(\ell)}\big),
\qquad
\bar d_B \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{\ell}\big[\,d^{(\ell)}_B\,\big],
\qquad
E_B \;=\; \Cov_{\ell}\big[\,d^{(\ell)}_B\,\big] .
\label{eq:Ec}
\end{equation}
$E_B$ is $K_B\times K_B$ by construction, and the two pieces are not comparable
in size. $\widehat g$ errs by a fixed amount at a fixed $\vartheta$, so within one
cloud that error is common to its patients and passes through a statistic
undiminished, while resampling $z^{(\ell)}$ moves only how the error varies across
the cloud. Narrowing the cloud shrinks $V^{\text{boot}}_B$ and the fluctuation
together and leaves $\bar d_B$ where it was, so the emulator's share of $V_B$
grows as $\omega$ falls: on this corpus the whole difference runs from $0.6$
sampling standard deviations at a cloud as wide as the stage-1 prior to $10$ at a
tenth of that width, with $\bar d_B$ taking an increasing share of it. Reporting
$E_B$ alone therefore understates $V_B$ by whatever $\bar d_B$ is, and nothing in
the fit registers the difference.

So $\bar d_B$ would have to be removed rather than carried, if it were large.
Measured against a training pool that covers the operating point, it is not.

\paragraph{Why there is no emulator correction.} An earlier draft specified an
affine correction $\psi(\widehat g_{\mathrm{c}}) = \psi(\widehat g) - \alpha -
\Gamma(\vartheta-\mu_0)$, fitted over $n_g$ solved patients drawn at $\phi_0$.
It was built, and it is not needed: the whole of $\bar d_B$ it was meant to
remove was an artifact of a design drawn where the fit does not read. Once the
design is the mixture above, the uncorrected emulator agrees with the simulator
to $0.4\%$ on readout IQR over the same patients, the correction buys $0.002$ in
held-out readout correlation, and that gain is flat across four orders of
magnitude in the ridge penalty --- so it is a constant offset, with no $\Gamma$
worth fitting. Against $\sqrt{E_B}/\sqrt{V^{\text{boot}}_B}$ of $0.02$, there is
nothing left to correct.

The lesson is worth keeping even though the machinery is not. An emulator
correction fitted at the wrong place is indistinguishable from one that is
needed: both report a large residual and both reduce it. What decides between
them is whether the design covers the evaluation measure, which is a property of
the pool and not of the correction.

The fallback is $E_B=\diag(\varepsilon^2)$, with $\varepsilon_r$ the held-out
residual of readout $r$ repeated across its $K_{rc}$ statistics. It asserts that
the emulator errs independently across readouts, which readouts sharing a species
do not satisfy, and that a readout error enters a scale statistic with the same
loading as a location statistic, where the true loading is near zero.

\paragraph{Where $\widehat g$ is trained.} $E_B$ and $\bar d_B$ are properties of
$\widehat g$, and $\widehat g$ is a property of the parameter values it was
trained on. So that training design is an input to the model, not an
implementation detail.

The fit calls the emulator at $\vartheta_i = \mu + D_\omega L_R z_i$, at one
$\phi$ per evaluation. Do not draw the design from the union over $\phi$, of
covariance $\Sigma_1 + \Omega$: in $P$ dimensions a Gaussian holds its mass on a
shell at $\sqrt{P} \pm 1/\sqrt{2}$ and none in its own interior, so such a design
has no rows near any particular $\phi$, and least of all near $\phi_0$, that
being the centre. Widening does not repair this; it moves the shell further from
where the emulator is read.

Draw a \emph{mixture} instead: a patient cloud about each of a few centres, one
of them $(\mu_0, \omega_0)$ exactly, so the operating point is covered whatever
the others do. Hedge for where the sampler travels by adding
components, never by widening one. Every parameter has to vary: a design that
pinned one would leave the likelihood undefined along it rather than merely flat.

Score the emulator on a held-out draw from the same law.

\paragraph{Assembling $V_B$.} Since reported uncertainties go to width rather
than to the error bar, both the scales and the correlations come from the
bootstrap and there is nothing to assemble:
\begin{equation}
V_B \;=\; V^{\text{boot}}_B \;+\; E_B .
\label{eq:Vsplit}
\end{equation}
Sampling noise from the predicted cloud, emulator error on top. A ridge
$\epsilon I$ is added before factoring, since two readouts of one cohort sharing
a denominator make $V_B$ near singular.

The cost is that $V_B$ now rests entirely on that cloud. A cloud that is too
narrow makes every error bar too small, the likelihood then overrides the prior,
and nothing in the fit catches it. Check first, against any source that reports a
sampling uncertainty $U_{rck}$ for row $(r,c,k)$ on the log scale:
\begin{equation}
\varrho_{rck} \;=\; U_{rck}\Big/\sqrt{\big(V^{\text{boot}}_c\big)_{rck,rck}} .
\label{eq:ratio}
\end{equation}
This is the residual of a standard-error row taken at $\phi_0$ instead of at
$\phi$, so computing it before fitting is a prior predictive check on the rows
that now carry $\omega$. Above one the cloud is too narrow at that readout. Fix
the model rather than widening $V_B$, since widening hides what is being tested.

\paragraph{One number, one job.} A reported uncertainty could either set the
error bar on its row or say something about population width. It cannot do both,
since that counts one number twice. It always goes to width.

The reason is scarcity. Numbers that speak to width are rare, and no source
reports a between-patient spread, so the width rows are all $\omega$ has. Sampling noise is abundant by
comparison, because \eqref{eq:V} supplies it on every row for nothing. So $U$
leaves $V_B$ and becomes a row of $\widehat T_B$, and $D_B$ always comes from the
bootstrap.

What is predicted depends on which number the source printed, so the extraction
still has to record that. A standard deviation is a population spread and is read
straight off $\tilde F_{rc}$. A standard error is a sampling spread, predicted at
$\phi$ rather than frozen at $\phi_0$. At $n_c=10$ the two differ by a factor of
$3.2$, so confusing them is not a small error.

A predicted standard error needs a smooth estimator. The closed forms serve for
most rows: $\mathrm{sd}/\sqrt{n_c}$ for a mean, and $1/(2f(m)\sqrt{n_c})$ for a
median, with $f$ the cloud's own density. Both are asymptotic and poor at the
smallest $n_c$, where a bootstrap of \eqref{eq:V} at $\phi$ is better. Cost runs
with $n_c$, so bootstrapping only the small cohorts is close to free. Freeze the
resample indices alongside $z_{1:N}$ so the row stays smooth in $\phi$.

Such a row buys precision on $\omega$ without touching the alias, since $\kappa_r$
scales a sampling standard deviation exactly as it scales an interquartile range.
It is approximate either way, because it depends on how the source built its
interval.

\paragraph{Choosing $\phi_0$.} Whether a plug-in is good enough is checkable:
rebuild $V_B$ at other $\phi_0$ and read how far the posterior moves. Section
\ref{sec:flat} supplies one alternative, $\phi_0=(\hat\mu_{\text{flat}},\
\omega_0)$, and it is not automatically the better one. Moving the plug-in moves
where $\widehat g$ is read, and the emulator's design covers some regions more
densely than others, so a plug-in closer to the truth can carry a larger
$\bar d$. Build $V_B$ at $(\mu_0,\omega_0)$ by default and use the flat plug-in
as the comparison.

\section{Priors}

\paragraph{Location.}
\begin{equation}
\mu \;\sim\; \Ncal_P\big(\mu_0,\ \Sigma_1\big),
\label{eq:mu}
\end{equation}
$\mu_0$ and $\Sigma_1$ the mean and covariance of the stage-1 composite prior on
the log scale. Recall that this is the stage 1- fitting in which we are specifically trying to learn the location parameter per QSP parameter.

Four parameters are bounded on $(0,1)$ and carry a logit rather than a lognormal
margin in \eqref{eq:crn}, so that no $\omega$ can place a patient outside the
bound. For those $\mu_j$ is the logit of the population median rather than its
log, so \eqref{eq:mu} is Gaussian as written and no draw can leave the bound. The
stage-1 marginal is moved into that coordinate before the fit, preserving the
median and carrying the spread by the delta method,
$d\,\mathrm{logit}(m)/d\log m = 1/(1-m)$, so a median near the bound widens most.

Keeping $\mu$ on the log scale and rejecting $\exp(\mu_j)\ge1$ instead does not
work. The rejection factor is piecewise constant, so it reads as free, but its
gradient is never consulted: a leapfrog step into the excluded region gives
infinite energy error and the trajectory is discarded as divergent. The four put
$33.7\%$ of the prior mass there jointly, and the chain diverged on $275$ of
$300$ draws. A bound the coordinate cannot violate has no wall to hit.

Note also that the role assigned to such a parameter no longer fixes its realised
spread: at $\omega=0.35$ a log margin gives a CV of $0.36$ at any median, while a
logit margin gives $0.075$ at median $0.79$ and $0.35$ at median $0.03$. A
maximum fractional suppression near its bound genuinely cannot vary as much as an
unbounded rate, so this is the bound doing its work rather than a distortion, but
the width a reader infers from the role is wrong for these four.

\paragraph{The spread centre.} $\omega_0$ is elicited by role
(\S\ref{sec:elicit}). A parameter may carry an explicit $\omega_{0j}$ in place of
its role, which none currently does.

\paragraph{Spread.} Every width is assumed. No source in the corpus reports a
between-patient spread, so
\begin{equation}
\log\omega_j \;=\; \log\omega_{0j} + s + u_j,
\qquad
s\sim\Ncal(0,\tau_s^2),
\qquad
u_j\sim\Ncal(0,\tau_u^2),
\qquad
\sum_{j} u_j = 0 .
\label{eq:omegaassumed}
\end{equation}
An earlier draft carved out a set $\Mset$ of measured widths, with its own
precision-weighted prior and a shrinkage of $\omega_0$ toward the role value.
Neither was ever exercised: $\Mset$ was empty on every corpus, the shrinkage was
never implemented, and carrying them cost the last simplification below its
sharpest statement. Their absence is what makes the $s$ / $b_1$ alias
unconditional rather than merely unbroken.
$s$ is how much wider or narrower the population is overall, and $u$ is the
pattern across parameters. Without the constraint those two overlap exactly: add
a constant to $s$, subtract it from every $u_j$, and $\omega$ is unchanged. The
constraint removes that flat direction and leaves $s$ as the only global level.

Keeping $s$ clean matters twice over. It is the quantity that reaches the virtual
population, and it is the one whose split against $b_1$ the last simplification
below is about.

In practice $u$ is drawn iid and its mean subtracted, which is the same
distribution as conditioning on the constraint.

\paragraph{Measurement discrepancy.} Free, because it has no parameter twin.
$Z$ has an intercept, so $a_1$ and $b_1$ are the global location and width
corrections.
\begin{equation}
a \;\sim\; \Ncal\big(0,\ \sigma_a^2 I\big),
\qquad
b \;\sim\; \Ncal\big(0,\ \sigma_b^2 I\big),
\qquad
\sigma_a=\sigma_b=0.5 .
\label{eq:abprior}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Mechanism discrepancy.} Tightly shrunk, because it competes with
$\mu$:
\begin{equation}
\beta_q \;=\; \tau_\beta\big(\beta^{\text{raw}}_q - \bar\beta^{\text{raw}}\big),
\qquad
\beta^{\text{raw}}_q \sim \Ncal(0,1),
\qquad
\tau_\beta = 0.15 \ \text{ fixed},
\label{eq:betaprior}
\end{equation}
for $q$ in a declared subset $\mathcal{S}\subset\{1,\dots,Q\}$, and $\beta_q=0$
outside it. The default $\mathcal{S}$ is the shared denominators,
$\mathrm{nucleated\_total}$ and $V_T$.

$\tau_\beta$ is fixed, not estimated. Estimating it creates a funnel, and with
$\beta$ centred there are too few free directions left for the hierarchy to pool
anything in return.

$\beta$ is centred to remove an exact alias. Suppose every species carried the
same bias, $\beta_q=c$. Then $e^c$ cancels out of every fraction and every ratio,
and shifts only the densities. The measurement map can produce exactly that, by
moving $\gamma$. So the common level of $\beta$ is invisible to the data, and the
prior alone would decide it. Centring takes it out.

\paragraph{Declared assay conversions.} Some readouts measure a different
quantity from the species they are matched to and say so: Herremans reports IL-10
in tumour homogenate, which is total, while $V_T.\mathrm{IL10}$ is the free pool,
so the readout is $R\cdot V_T.\mathrm{IL10}$ with $R\ge 1$ unknown. Write $\mathcal{A}$
for these,
\begin{equation}
\log R_m \;\sim\; \Ncal\big(\log R_{0m},\ \sigma_{Rm}^2\big),
\qquad m\in\mathcal{A} ,
\label{eq:auxprior}
\end{equation}
centred where the assay literature puts the conversion. They enter inside $h_r$,
the only term indexed by readout and species at once: $\beta$ is shared across
readouts, and $\gamma$ sits downstream and so cannot decompose through a ratio.
Nor could $\tau_\beta=0.15$ carry a $\log R$ of 1.6 to 3.9. Where a species has
both a free $\beta$ and a conversion with no readout separating them, only one
may be free.

\section{Posterior and deliverable}

\begin{equation}
\phi \;=\;
\big(\mu,\ s,\ u^{\text{raw}},\
a,\ b,\ \beta^{\text{raw}}_{\mathcal{S}},\ \log R_{\mathcal{A}}\big).
\end{equation}
$\mu$ is sampled as $\mu = \mu_0 + L_1\mu^{\text{raw}}$ with
$\Sigma_1 = L_1L_1\Tr$.

\begin{equation}
p(\phi\mid\Dset)
\;\propto\;
p(\phi)\;\prod_{c=1}^{C}\;
\Ncal\big(\widehat T_{B};\ \tau_{B}(\phi),\ V_{B}\big).
\label{eq:post}
\end{equation}

The virtual population is the posterior predictive over patients,
\begin{equation}
p(\theta_{\text{new}}\mid\Dset)
\;=\;
\int \Ncal_P\!\big(\log\theta_{\text{new}};\ \mu,\ \Omega\big)\;
p(\phi\mid\Dset)\;d\phi ,
\label{eq:vpop}
\end{equation}
and it is emitted as an ensemble: one population per posterior draw of $\phi$,
so that between-patient variability and uncertainty about $\phi$ stay separable
in whatever is computed downstream.

\section{The flat fit}
\label{sec:flat}

The flat fit estimates the centre of the population and holds its shape fixed.
Set $\omega=\omega_0$ and drop the three spread terms from the parameter vector:
\begin{equation}
\phi_{\text{flat}} \;=\;
\big(\mu,\ a,\ \beta^{\text{raw}}_{\mathcal{S}}\big),
\qquad
\omega=\omega_0,
\qquad
b=0 .
\label{eq:phiflat}
\end{equation}
Nothing else changes. Equations \eqref{eq:crn} to \eqref{eq:post} run as
written, with $\omega$ a constant rather than a function of $\phi$.

It is fitted by optimisation, with a Gauss-Newton covariance at the optimum
rather than a posterior sample. What this fit has to deliver is a point, the
plug-in, plus enough uncertainty in $\mu$ to carry into the width shortfall.
Both come out of that covariance.

\paragraph{Why $\omega_0$ and not zero.} Setting $\omega=0$ collapses the cloud
onto one point, and three things die with it. $\tilde F_c$ becomes a point mass,
so \eqref{eq:V} returns zero and $V_B$ has to come from outside the model.
$w^{(c)}$ becomes a switch on the whole cohort instead of on patients, so the
eligibility rule stops working and turns discontinuous in $\mu$. And every
location functional of a point mass returns the same number, so a cohort that
reports a mean beside a median predicts one value for two rows.

Each of those is a diagnostic, and diagnostics are what the flat fit is for. The
third is the sharpest: the gap between a reported mean and a reported median is
the skewness of the pushforward, which at $\omega=\omega_0$ the model predicts
rather than absorbs.

\paragraph{What it buys.}

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}lX@{}}
\toprule
$V_B$ is computable & \eqref{eq:V} resamples a real cloud, so the bootstrap works unchanged. \\
The width rows are held out & Predict them at $\omega_0$ and report the ratio per readout. That is the width shortfall, before any population parameter is estimated. \\
$\hat\mu$ needs no translation & It is already a population centre, so there is no Jensen gap to correct afterwards. \\
It supplies the plug-in & Take $\phi_0=(\hat\mu_{\text{flat}},\ \omega_0)$ for \eqref{eq:V}. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

Fit the centre rows and hold every width row out, so the ratio above is an
honest comparison. The centre rows are the medians and the means. A width row is
either one printed as a width, an $\sigma$ or an interquartile range, or a
quantile away from the median: a cohort reporting quartiles states its width
through the gap between them, so fitting $\hat q_{0.25}$ and $\hat q_{0.75}$
would fit the width and leave the ratio measuring a residual from a fit that had
already seen it. This is a choice of which rows to fit and not a re-expression of
any of them.

$b$ is pinned at zero here, because centre rows cannot see it. The pivot sits at
the median, so $\kappa_r$ drops out of a median row entirely, and it enters a
mean row only through the small gap between the mean and the median. It does
enter a quartile row, with multiplier $\hat q_p-c_{rc}$, which is the second
reason those are held out. $b$ is estimated in the population fit.

\paragraph{Cost.} A gradient step still costs $C\times N$ forward passes, but $N$
only has to resolve the location statistics and a rough width, not the flanks at
a precision that supports inference on $\omega$, so it sits far below the $N$ of
the population fit.

\paragraph{What it cannot test.} The shape of the population: $\omega$, $R$, and
the direction $s$ and $b_1$ share. It does test the prior on $\mu$, the forward
map, the discrepancy terms, the block structure of \eqref{eq:obs} and the
sampler, and a fault in any of those is one the population fit would also have.

\section{What the rows determine}
\label{sec:determined}

$\phi$ has far more coordinates than there are rows, so most of the posterior is
the stage-1 prior handed back. That is the design rather than a failure. Stage 1
exists to supply what these rows cannot. What is worth knowing is which
directions the rows did move, since that is what separates the two.

Linearise $\tau$ at the plug-in, whiten by $V$, and standardise by the prior:
\begin{equation}
G \;=\; I + J\Tr J,
\qquad
J \;=\; V^{-1/2}\,\frac{\partial\tau}{\partial\phi}\,\Sigma_\phi^{1/2} .
\label{eq:ginfo}
\end{equation}
$J$ has $K$ rows, so $J\Tr J$ has rank at most $K$, and at least $\dim\phi - K$
of its eigenvalues are zero. Along one of those the posterior precision is $I$,
which is the prior. The reason is direct: if $Jv=0$ then moving $\phi$ along $v$
leaves every predicted row exactly where it was, so no data at these rows could
distinguish the two.

Mixing in the forward map does not change this. It decides which combinations
are constrained, not how many.

This is a statement about the eigenbasis and not about single parameters. A given
$\mu_j$ projects partly onto informed directions and partly onto flat ones, so it
usually returns a little narrower than its prior rather than identical. It is
also local: the flat directions turn as $\phi$ moves. Report the spectrum of $G$
and the split of $\mathrm{tr}(J\Tr J)$ across the blocks of $\phi$.

Two consequences follow from the shape alone.

The measurement map takes most of the information. $a$ has a handful of
coefficients against hundreds of mechanism parameters, and it acts directly on
the rows where the mechanism has to act through the simulator. So these rows
mostly estimate $a$. Whatever the model can route into $a$ will end up there, the
emulator offset of \S\ref{sec:checks} included.

Row space is saturated. When $\mathrm{rank}(J)$ equals the number of rows, any
question of the form "can the model produce this row effect" is answered yes by
construction. That is why \eqref{eq:zcost} asks what an effect costs instead of
whether it is reachable.

The spectrum also says what the sampler faces. A wide spread means trajectories
run much further along the loosest direction than the tightest, which warmup will
not find on its own. $G^{-1}$ at the mode is cheap and can be given to the
sampler as its metric.

\section{Checks}
\label{sec:checks}

Three quantities to compute before the population fit is believed. All are cheap
next to the fit itself.

\paragraph{Is the predicted cloud wide enough?} The ratio $\varrho_{rck}$ of
\eqref{eq:ratio} at $\phi_0$, and the same comparison in \S\ref{sec:flat} against
width rows no fit has seen.

\paragraph{Can the measurement map hold the emulator's offset?} To first order at
$a=b=0$ the map moves a row by
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial\tau_{rck}}{\partial a} = Z_r\Tr\,\mathbb{1}\{k \text{ location}\},
\qquad
\frac{\partial\tau_{rck}}{\partial b} = Z_r\Tr\,\mathbb{1}\{k \text{ scale}\},
\label{eq:absorb}
\end{equation}
a common shift leaving an interquartile range alone, and \eqref{eq:disc} pivoting
where a location statistic sits. Stack these over all $K=\sum_c K_c$ rows into
$W$ and report how much of $\bar d$ survives projection onto them, whitened:
\begin{equation}
\text{captured} \;=\;
\big\|P_{V^{-1/2}W}\;V^{-1/2}\bar d\big\|
\;\Big/\;
\big\|V^{-1/2}\bar d\big\| .
\label{eq:absorbfrac}
\end{equation}
High says the emulator's bias is representable, and paid for in $a$. Low says it
is sitting in $\mu$.

\paragraph{Does $Z$ have work of its own?} \eqref{eq:disc} asserts that assay
modality and quantity type have no mechanistic twin. Asking whether the mechanism
\emph{can} reproduce the row effect of a column of $Z$ is vacuous at $P\gg K$,
since $\partial\tau/\partial(\mu,\omega)$ then spans row space and the residual is
zero whatever $Z$ is. Ask what it costs instead:
\begin{equation}
\delta^{\ast}_j \;=\; \arg\min_{\delta}\ \delta\Tr\Sigma^{-1}\delta
\qquad\text{subject to}\qquad
J_{\mu\omega}\,\delta \;=\; W e_j ,
\label{eq:zcost}
\end{equation}
with $W e_j$ the row effect of a unit coefficient on column $j$, $J_{\mu\omega}$
the Jacobian of $\tau$ in the mechanism, and $\Sigma$ the prior covariance of
$(\mu,\log\omega)$. Report $\|\delta^{\ast}_j\|$ in prior standard deviations.
Large means only an implausible mechanism move imitates that column, so it is
identified against the mechanism. Small means the two are confounded and the
priors decide the split.

\section{Known simplifications}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Independence across blocks.} Equation \eqref{eq:obs} asserts that
distinct blocks are distinct patients. Shared patients are handled inside a
block, so what remains is that no two publications overlap, which registry
sources do not guarantee.

The within-block correlation is predicted rather than measured, since a study
reporting order statistics at two timepoints does not report their joint. Where
per-patient values are recoverable, as digitised paired plots often make them,
$V^{\text{boot}}_B$ can be taken from the reported patients instead.

Report the effective row count $K_B/\{1+(K_B-1)\bar\rho_B\}$ at mean off-diagonal
correlation $\bar\rho_B$. Rows can outnumber patients: one source supplies 20 of
the 54 targets from about ten patients per arm, and there
$V^{\text{boot}}_B$ is singular and only $E_B$ makes $V_B$ invertible.

\item \textbf{The study offset is shrunk, not free.} A free $\eta_s$ is aliased
with the measurement discrepancy along $8$ directions. Equation
\eqref{eq:studyeff} fixes $\tau_\eta$ instead, so how much of a between-study
difference is called heterogeneity rather than signal is asserted. Only $7$ of
the $16$ occupied (quantity type, assay modality) cells are reported by more than
one study, so the corpus constrains $\tau_\eta$ weakly. Report the fit at more
than one $\tau_\eta$.

The single-row argument for fixing it has weakened: $3$ of the $22$ studies now
report one row, against $13$ when this was written. The aliasing against $Z$ is
what still carries the decision, and a free $\eta_s$ is worth re-testing.

\item \textbf{$V$ frozen at $\phi_0$.} Equation \eqref{eq:post} is an estimating
equation, not a likelihood. Letting $V$ move with $\phi$ would reintroduce a
$-\tfrac12\log\det V$ term that rewards a narrow population regardless of fit.

\item \textbf{$s$ and $b_1$ share a direction.} When the fitted population comes
out narrow there are two explanations, and the data do not separate them: the
patients really are alike, or the simulator under-disperses and $\kappa$ has
stretched the readouts to compensate. Only $s$ reaches the virtual population,
since $b_1$ is discarded with the rest of the measurement map, so the split
changes what gets shipped.

Every width row loads on $s$ and on $b_1$ identically, so only their sum is
identified and no row can separate them. This is a property of the model as
written, not a shortage in the corpus: a readout could help only if the share of
its across-patient variance coming from measured widths differed from the rest,
and \eqref{eq:omegaassumed} leaves no width measured.

So $\tau_s$ and $\sigma_b$ decide the split, and in proportion to their
variances: $b_1$ takes $\sigma_b^2/(\tau_s^2+\sigma_b^2) = 73\%$ of any width
excess and is discarded, $s$ takes the remaining $27\%$ and is what ships. At the
values in the table that ratio is an accident of two numbers chosen separately.
It is also directional, so the shipped population is narrower than the width rows
indicate by roughly a factor of four in the excess. Report the joint posterior of
the pair, and treat the pair of priors as the modelling decision it is.

The cheapest resolution is an identification restriction rather than more data:
pinning $b_1=0$ asserts that there is no global assay stretch and sends the whole
excess to $s$, which is a statable claim and can be argued with.

Measurement error is already carried here by $\kappa$, which matches an inflated
spread without saying where it came from. Giving it its own term would load on
the same rows as $b_1$ and make the tie three-way. A prior on it would break the
tie, and an assay coefficient of variation is easier to come by than a measured
between-patient width.

\item \textbf{$Z$ is asserted, not tested.} The claim that assay modality and
quantity type have no mechanistic twin, while species-level bias does, is an
argument rather than a result. Projecting a column of $Z$ onto
$\mathrm{col}\big(\partial\tau/\partial(\mu,\omega)\big)$ does not test it, for
the reason \S\ref{sec:checks} gives. \eqref{eq:zcost} is the version that
survives the dimensions, and it has not been run.
\end{itemize}
